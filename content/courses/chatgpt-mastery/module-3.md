## Section 1: The AI Communication Framework

Before diving into specific communication types, understand the meta-framework that applies to all professional AI-assisted writing.

### The Three-Layer Communication Model

Every piece of professional communication operates on three layers:

**Layer 1: Content** — What you're saying (facts, ideas, recommendations, data)
**Layer 2: Structure** — How it's organized (the sequence, the emphasis, the format)
**Layer 3: Voice** — How it sounds (tone, vocabulary, style, personality)

AI excels at Layer 1 and Layer 2. Layer 3 requires your active involvement.

The professional AI writing process:
1. You bring the content knowledge and the professional judgment
2. AI helps structure and draft the content with appropriate format
3. You edit for your voice, your specific relationships, and your organizational context

Never use AI output without passing it through your own voice filter. The AI does not know how you and your CEO relate, what inside references will land, or what tone is right for your specific reader.

### The Voice Calibration Prompt

Use this prompt to teach an AI your voice before any significant writing task:

```prompt
Before I give you my writing task, I want you to learn my writing voice 
so the output sounds like me, not like generic AI.

Here are three examples of my writing — emails or other documents that 
capture how I actually communicate at work:

EXAMPLE 1:
[paste example]

EXAMPLE 2:
[paste example]

EXAMPLE 3:
[paste example]

Analyze my writing style and identify:
1. My typical sentence length and rhythm
2. My vocabulary choices (simple/technical/formal/casual)
3. How I open and close messages
4. How I handle uncertainty or hedging
5. Any distinctive patterns or phrases

Then confirm you understand my voice before I give you the writing task.
```

> 💡 **Pro Tip:** Save the voice analysis the AI produces about your writing. It becomes a portable description you can paste into future prompts: "Write this in my voice. Here is an analysis of my style: [paste analysis]." This saves time and produces consistently better style matching.

## Section 2: Email Excellence

### Crafting Difficult Emails

The most valuable AI communication assistance is often for difficult, high-stakes emails — the ones you agonize over for 30 minutes before sending.

**Delivering bad news:**
```prompt
I need to write an email to a client telling them that a project will be 
delivered 3 weeks late. This is the second delay on this project.

Context:
- Client: Jennifer Walsh, Chief Marketing Officer at Hartfield Retail
- Our relationship: 2-year client, generally positive but I know she's frustrated
- The cause of this delay: A key team member had an unexpected medical leave 
  AND our third-party data vendor had a service outage that set us back 8 days
- What we CAN do: Deliver the first phase on the original date, just not the full project
- What we're offering: 15% discount on the delayed portion and a dedicated 
  project lead for the remainder

Write an email that:
- Opens with the news directly (no buried lede)
- Takes appropriate accountability without over-explaining or making excuses
- Factually mentions the external factors without using them as justifications
- Clearly describes the split delivery option as a positive alternative
- Mentions the discount as a gesture of goodwill, not a negotiation opener
- Ends with confidence and a clear next step

Tone: Accountable, calm, solution-focused. Jennifer is direct herself — 
don't over-soften this.
Length: Under 200 words.
```

**Handling conflict:**
```prompt
I received this email from a colleague that feels passive-aggressive and 
I want to respond professionally without escalating the situation:

[paste the email]

Help me draft a response that:
- Addresses the substance of their concern directly
- Doesn't match their tone (I want to de-escalate)
- Is clear about my perspective without being defensive
- Opens a path to a real conversation if the issue is more significant 
  than an email exchange can resolve
- Does not include sarcasm, passive-aggressiveness, or anything I'd regret

After the draft, tell me: is there a better approach than email for this 
situation? Should I pick up the phone instead?
```

### Executive Communication

Writing to or for executives requires a different calibration. Executives are busy, direct, and results-focused.

**The five rules of executive communication:**
1. Lead with the conclusion, not the background
2. Numbers anchor every claim
3. Options come with your recommendation, not just options
4. Ask for exactly what you need (not "thoughts")
5. Total length under 250 words unless they asked for more

```prompt
I need to send a weekly project status update to our CEO.

The facts:
- Project: Customer portal migration
- Status: On schedule (Week 6 of 14)
- This week's milestone: Completed user authentication module (2 days ahead of schedule)
- Risk: Our third-party payment integration vendor just changed their API — 
  our dev team estimates 2-3 days of rework needed in Week 9
- Budget: Currently 3% under budget ($180K of $185K projected for this point)
- Ask: Approval to begin user acceptance testing recruitment now (ahead of schedule)
  so we don't lose time in Week 11

Write a 150-word CEO status update that:
- Leads with our current status (on track)
- Notes the payment API risk and how we're handling it
- Mentions the budget position (brief)
- Ends with a clear approval request for UAT recruitment

Do not pad this. Every sentence should carry information.
```

## Section 3: Proposals and Business Cases

Proposals are where business is won and lost. AI dramatically accelerates the drafting process — but your strategy and relationship knowledge must drive the content.

### The Proposal Framework

A winning proposal answers these five questions in this order:

1. Do you understand my problem? (Empathy and diagnosis)
2. Is your solution the right one? (Approach and fit)
3. Can you deliver? (Proof and credibility)
4. What will it cost and what will I get? (Investment and ROI)
5. What do I do next? (Clear call to action)

```prompt
I'm writing a proposal for a content strategy and execution retainer 
for a B2B cybersecurity software company.

Client context from our discovery call:
- Company: SecureVault, 120 employees, selling to enterprise IT departments
- Their problem: They've doubled their sales team but marketing content hasn't kept up
- Their goal: Need 3x more content output, specifically case studies, technical 
  white papers, and sales enablement materials
- Their concern: Past agencies gave them generic content that didn't resonate with 
  their technical buyers
- Budget: $12,000-15,000/month
- Timeline: Want to start next month

Our offering:
- Team: 2 senior writers (one has IT/cybersecurity background)
- Process: Technical discovery sessions before each piece; all content reviewed 
  by their SMEs
- Deliverables: 4 case studies, 2 white papers, 1 sales deck update per month

Write a proposal that:
- Opens by demonstrating we understood what they told us (not generic opener)
- Clearly addresses their specific concern about technical resonance
- Presents our deliverables with brief rationale for each
- Includes a simple monthly investment and what they get
- Closes with next steps (signing, kickoff timeline)

Format: Clean, scannable with headers. Under 700 words. Professional but not stiff.
```

### Responding to RFPs

RFPs (Requests for Proposals) are structured documents with specific requirements. AI helps enormously with these:

```prompt
I need to respond to this RFP section by section. Here is the RFP document:

[paste RFP]

For each section:
1. Identify what the evaluator is really asking (beyond the surface question)
2. What does a strong response to this section look like vs. a weak one?
3. Draft our response based on our capabilities [describe your capabilities]

Start with Section 1 and we'll work through each section together.
```

## Section 4: Internal Reports and Documentation

### The Status Report That Gets Read

Most status reports are written for the writer's comfort, not the reader's efficiency. Here is the structure that executives actually read:

**BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front) — 2 sentences max**
**Current Status** — where we are against plan (traffic light: Green/Yellow/Red)
**Key Accomplishments** — what was completed this period (specific, not vague)
**Risks and Issues** — what might derail us (honest, with mitigation)
**Next Period Focus** — what we're doing next (tied to milestones)
**Ask** — if you need a decision or resource

```prompt
Here are my project notes for this week:

[paste raw notes]

Format these as a professional weekly status report using the BLUF structure:
- Bottom Line Up Front (2 sentences: status and single most important thing to know)
- Current Status (Red/Yellow/Green with one-line explanation)
- Key Accomplishments This Week (bullet points, specific)
- Risks and Issues (bullet points with severity and mitigation)
- Next Week Focus (bullet points)
- Decisions Needed (if any)

Keep it scannable — this goes to 3 VPs who will spend 60 seconds reading it.
```

### Meeting Notes That Capture What Matters

Raw meeting notes are valuable; structured meeting documentation is valuable to everyone on the team. AI bridges this gap:

```prompt
Here are the raw transcript/notes from our 60-minute product strategy meeting:

[paste notes or transcript]

Create professional meeting documentation:

MEETING: [Date, Title, Attendees]

DECISIONS MADE:
[List each decision with brief context — what was decided, not the full discussion]

ACTION ITEMS:
| Action | Owner | Due Date | Dependencies |

DISCUSSION SUMMARY:
[3-4 bullet points capturing the main threads of discussion — key insights and concerns]

OPEN ITEMS / PARKING LOT:
[Things raised but not resolved]

NEXT MEETING:
[Date, purpose, who should attend]

Note: Where the notes are ambiguous about who owns an action item, flag it 
with [CLARIFY OWNER] rather than guessing.
```

## Section 5: Diplomacy and Nuance — What AI Needs Human Help With

There are communication situations where AI gives you a starting point but your human judgment is absolutely essential. Recognizing these situations is part of professional AI mastery.

### Situations Requiring Heavy Human Editing

**When power dynamics are complex:** An email to your boss's boss, a message to a board member, or a difficult communication across organizational levels requires you to know the specific relationships and unwritten rules. AI can draft; you must calibrate.

**When emotion is central:** Condolence messages, acknowledgments of personal difficulty, or communications about sensitive employee situations require genuine personal voice and care. An AI draft might be structurally correct but emotionally hollow.

**When organizational politics are in play:** Internal communications about budget battles, team conflicts, or politically charged decisions require you to navigate subtext that only you understand.

**When legal risk exists:** Any communication that could create legal obligations, admissions of liability, or documentation in potential disputes should be reviewed by legal counsel — not just edited for voice.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Sending AI-generated apology communications or condolences with only light editing. Readers often sense the generic quality of AI-generated empathy, which is worse than a brief but genuine personal message. For high-emotion situations, use AI to organize your thoughts and then rewrite the entire message in your own words.

### The Human-AI Communication Collaboration Model

Think of AI as your first-draft colleague who is excellent at structure and competent at tone, but needs you to provide:

- The specific relationships and history
- The organizational context and politics
- The genuine emotional intelligence and care
- The personal voice that makes the communication authentically yours
- The professional judgment about what to say and what to leave unsaid

```prompt
I need to communicate something sensitive and I want to think it through 
before writing anything.

Situation: [describe the situation]

Before drafting anything, help me think through:
1. What does the recipient most need to hear from this communication?
2. What does the recipient most fear hearing?
3. What should NOT be in writing, and why?
4. What tone will most likely achieve the outcome I want?
5. Is there anything in my situation description that I should reconsider 
   before I write this at all?
```

## Section 6: Building Communication Templates for Your Role

The most efficient professionals today have AI-assisted template systems for the communications they do most often.

### Template System Architecture

**Tier 1 — Fully Templated (AI generates, light editing):**
- Meeting recaps
- Routine status updates
- Standard client check-ins
- Internal announcements of decisions already made

**Tier 2 — AI-Assisted (AI drafts, significant editing):**
- Proposals and pitches
- Difficult client communications
- Executive-level requests
- Performance feedback documents

**Tier 3 — AI-Informed (AI supports thinking, human writes):**
- Sensitive HR communications
- Negotiations
- Crisis communications
- Personal notes and relationship maintenance

### Building Your Template Library

```prompt
I send roughly [type of communication] approximately [X times per week].

Here's an example of one I've written that I was happy with:
[paste example]

Create a reusable template with:
- [PLACEHOLDER] variables clearly labeled for what changes each time
- Fixed language that I like from my example, kept as-is
- A brief header note on when to use this template and what to customize
- Three variations for different contexts: [describe contexts]
```

> 🔍 **Case Study:** A VP of Customer Success at a B2B software company manages 45 enterprise accounts. She built a 12-template communication library with AI covering: quarterly business review prep emails, renewal outreach (3 variations by health score), escalation acknowledgment, executive introduction requests, and reference requests. Her team of 4 uses the shared library. Building the library took 4 hours. They estimate it saves 8+ hours per week across the team and has measurably improved response rates on renewal outreach (18% higher than before the templates).

## Key Takeaways

1. **AI excels at content and structure; you own voice** — the three-layer model defines the collaboration: AI builds the scaffolding, you bring the relationship and personality.

2. **Voice calibration before writing tasks** — teach the AI your style with examples before important communications; the output quality difference is significant.

3. **Executive communication has its own rules** — conclusion first, numbers in every claim, recommendations with options, total length under 250 words for most messages.

4. **Proposals win on empathy and specificity** — AI helps you structure winning proposals, but you must bring genuine understanding of the client's real problem.

5. **Internal documentation is underrated** — structured meeting notes, BLUF status reports, and well-organized documentation make you more visible as a high-quality professional.

6. **Know when AI needs heavy human editing** — political situations, emotional content, legal risk, and personal relationships require significant human judgment and voice.

7. **Templates compound over time** — a communication template library is an asset that pays for itself weekly; build one deliberately.
