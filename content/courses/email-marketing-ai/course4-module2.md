## Section 1: Subject Lines — The Single Most Important Element

### The Psychology of Subject Lines

A subject line has one job: get the email opened. Nothing else matters if the email isn't opened.

Subject lines work through psychological mechanisms. Understanding these mechanisms lets you write (and prompt AI to write) subject lines that actually work:

**Curiosity Gap:** Creates an information gap that the brain wants to close. "I made a mistake last year (and it cost me $40,000)" — you have to open it to find out what happened.

**Self-Interest:** Directly states a specific benefit the reader gets by opening. "Your January today tax deductions (a checklist)" — opens because of clear value.

**Social Proof:** References others' experiences or widespread behavior. "What 4,200 marketers said about AI tools (the surprising answer)"

**Urgency/Scarcity:** Creates time pressure that motivates action. "Tomorrow is the last day: [offer]" — only works if the urgency is genuine.

**Personalization:** Using specific information about the reader. "[First Name], based on what you bought last month..." — works because it signals relevance.

**Contrarianism:** Challenges conventional wisdom. "Why your email open rate doesn't matter" — works because it creates cognitive dissonance.

### Generating High-Converting Subject Lines

```prompt
Write 20 subject line options for an email about [topic/offer].

Email content summary: [what the email is actually about]
Primary audience: [who is receiving this]
The main benefit of opening this email: [what they get]
The emotion I want them to feel when they see this: [curiosity / excitement / 
  urgency / trust / curiosity / nostalgia]
Tone: [professional / casual / playful / direct / warm]
Brand voice: [description]

Generate 20 subject lines using different psychological approaches:
- 4 curiosity gap subject lines
- 4 direct benefit subject lines
- 4 story/personal experience subject lines
- 3 social proof or data-led subject lines
- 3 contrarian or unexpected subject lines
- 2 question-format subject lines

After generating all 20, tell me:
- Your top 3 and why
- Which 3 to A/B test against each other first
- Any that you'd remove entirely and why
```

### Before/After Subject Line Comparison

**Weak subject lines (and why they fail):**

| Weak Subject Line | Problem |
|-----------------|---------|
| "Newsletter #47" | Zero value proposition; no reason to open |
| "Check out our latest products!" | Vague, self-serving, no benefit to reader |
| "Important update from [Company]" | Overused and creates no curiosity |
| "Happy Holidays from our team" | Generic, everyone sends this |
| "You're going to love this" | Hyperbolic and unspecific |

**Strong subject lines (and why they work):**

| Strong Subject Line | Why It Works |
|-------------------|-------------|
| "The 3 emails I never should have sent (and what I learned)" | Story + curiosity + lesson |
| "Your order just shipped — here's what to do while you wait" | Relevant, timely, extra value |
| "I was wrong about [thing you care about]" | Curiosity + vulnerability + relevance |
| "What 1,847 of your peers said about [topic]" | Specific social proof |
| "Only 12 hours left — [specific offer]" | Urgency (only works if true) |

## Section 2: Email Structure and Psychology

### The Email Reading Pattern

Understanding how people actually read emails changes how you structure them:

1. **From name** — first thing seen; if they don't recognize you, many delete without reading further
2. **Subject line** — decides open/delete
3. **Preview text** — 40-80 characters below the subject line in many email clients; often ignored in writing but crucial for opens
4. **First sentence** — if this doesn't hook, they may close without reading
5. **First paragraph** — establishes if this email is worth their time
6. **Scan for value** — subheads, bullet points, bold text — they scan before committing to read
7. **CTA** — what you want them to do (must be clear and easy)

Most email writers put their best thinking in the middle of the email. Most readers never get there.

### The EMAIL Framework for Structure

**E — Engage:** Open with the hook that makes them read the next sentence
**M — Message:** The core content or story you're sharing
**A — Application:** How this applies to them specifically
**I — Invitation:** The bridge from content to action
**L — Link/CTA:** The clear, specific action you want

```prompt
Write a promotional email for [product/offer] using the EMAIL framework.

Product: [description]
Offer details: [price, discount, deadline if applicable]
Primary audience: [who is receiving this]
Their main problem this product solves: [the pain point]
Key objection they might have to buying: [what holds them back]
What makes this offer compelling right now: [the reason to act now]

EMAIL Framework:
E — Engage: Open with a story or scenario that immediately puts them 
    in the mindset of someone who needs this solution
M — Message: Share the key thing they need to know about this product
A — Application: Show specifically how their life/work changes with this product
I — Invitation: Bridge from the content to the offer naturally (not a hard pivot)
L — Link: Clear CTA with urgency and easy next step

Length: 350-450 words
Tone: [your brand voice]
P.S.: Include a P.S. that restates the most compelling element or deadline 
      (P.S. lines are read more than body copy in most emails)
```

## Section 3: Writing Different Email Types

### The Newsletter

Newsletters are relationship builders, not salespeople. The best newsletters today have a clear editorial voice, consistent format, and give more than they ask.

```prompt
Write an edition of my email newsletter for [week/month/topic].

Newsletter context:
- Newsletter name: [name]
- What my newsletter is about: [focus]
- My audience: [description]
- Newsletter format: [describe your usual format — curated links / 
  original essay / mix / etc.]
- This week's main topic or theme: [describe]

Content I want to include:
1. Lead story: [what you want to write about mainly]
2. Secondary item: [a resource, link, or quick thought]
3. Recommendation: [a tool, book, or resource to share]
4. Community/personal touch: [something more personal or community-focused]

Write this newsletter with:
- An opening that doesn't begin with "Hey [audience]! This week I'm sharing..."
- Your editorial perspective woven throughout (not just summaries)
- Conversational transitions between sections
- A closing that leaves them looking forward to the next edition
- Total length: 600-800 words

Voice: This should sound like a person with a perspective and personality, 
not a content aggregation service.
```

### The Nurture Email

Nurture emails move subscribers from awareness to consideration without hard-selling. They work by consistently demonstrating that you understand the subscriber's problem and know how to solve it.

```prompt
Write a nurture email for subscribers in the consideration stage — 
they know about my brand but haven't purchased yet.

My product: [description]
The subscriber's context: They've been on my list for 3-4 weeks, 
opened several emails, but haven't visited my product page yet.
Their likely objection to buying: [what usually holds people back]
The thing I want to share: [a case study / helpful tip / relevant story / 
framework that demonstrates my expertise]

Write an email that:
- Opens with a relatable scenario or question (not "I wanted to share something...")
- Delivers genuine value in the content (not just teasing it)
- Naturally connects the content to my product without feeling like bait-and-switch
- Ends with a soft CTA — inviting, not pressuring
- Makes them feel like they're getting value whether or not they buy

Length: 250-350 words. This email should feel like receiving advice from someone 
who knows your situation, not a marketing email.
```

### The Launch/Promotional Sequence

Product launches and promotions require a sequence, not a single email. Here's how to plan and execute one:

```prompt
I'm launching [product/offer] and need a 5-email launch sequence.

Launch details:
- Product: [description]
- Price: [amount]
- Launch period: [dates — open cart from [date] to [date]]
- What's included: [key features/benefits]
- Who it's for: [specific audience description]
- Primary transformation/result: [what they get from it]
- Social proof available: [testimonials, case studies, early results]
- Reason to buy now (scarcity/urgency): [real reason]

Plan a 5-email sequence:

EMAIL 1 (Launch day): The announcement — lead with the transformation, 
not the product. Make them want the result before introducing the offer.

EMAIL 2 (Day 2): The story — share a customer success story or your own 
experience that shows the product's impact in a concrete, specific way.

EMAIL 3 (Day 3 or 4): Address objections — tackle the 2-3 most common 
reasons people hesitate, directly and honestly.

EMAIL 4 (Closing day morning): Value reminder + FAQ — remind them what 
they get and answer questions that indicate genuine interest but uncertainty.

EMAIL 5 (Closing day evening, 3-4 hours before close): Last chance — 
urgency email that is direct and honest about the deadline.

For each email, provide: subject line, preview text, full copy (300-400 words each).
Tone: [your brand voice]
Note: Every email should stand alone for people who missed earlier ones.
```

## Section 4: Adapting AI Output to Your Voice

### The Voice Editing Process

AI-generated email copy is rarely perfect on the first draft. It typically needs voice editing — the process of making it sound unmistakably like you rather than a capable but generic AI.

**The five common AI voice problems:**

1. **Over-formal openers:** "I hope this finds you well" / "I'm reaching out today because"
2. **Vague generalizations:** "Many professionals struggle with..." / "In today's fast-paced world..."
3. **Predictable structure:** Every email becomes an obvious 3-part essay
4. **Missing personality:** No humor, no vulnerability, no distinctive perspective
5. **Generic CTAs:** "Click here to learn more" / "Don't miss out on this opportunity"

**The voice editing checklist:**

After generating an AI email draft, read through and check each:
- [ ] Does the first sentence sound like something I would actually say?
- [ ] Is there a moment of genuine personality — a perspective, an opinion, or a quirk?
- [ ] Have I replaced every "many professionals" with a specific example?
- [ ] Is the CTA specific enough that someone knows exactly what happens when they click?
- [ ] Does this sound like the emails I'm known for, or like generic email marketing?

```prompt
Here is an AI-generated email draft:

[paste the draft]

Edit this draft to sound more like my voice. Here are 3 examples of my 
writing that capture my style:

EXAMPLE 1: [paste]
EXAMPLE 2: [paste]
EXAMPLE 3: [paste]

Specifically:
1. Rewrite the opening line so it doesn't begin generically
2. Add one moment of specific personal voice (something only I could say)
3. Replace any vague phrases with specific examples
4. Rewrite the CTA to be more direct and specific
5. Remove any phrases that sound like marketing jargon

Show me both the original and the edited version so I can see what changed.
```

## Section 5: Preview Text — The Hidden Conversion Lever

Preview text is the 40-100 characters shown below the subject line in most email clients. It's arguably the second most important element of whether someone opens your email — and almost nobody thinks about it deliberately.

**Bad preview text (defaults or neglected):**
- "View this email in your browser" (never do this)
- The first sentence of the email (often context-less and weak)
- Empty (appears as the first sentence by default)

**Good preview text:**
- Completes the subject line thought
- Adds the secondary benefit or urgency element
- Teases what's inside without giving it away
- Functions as a "second subject line"

```prompt
I've written these subject lines for my email:

Subject Line 1: [subject line]
Subject Line 2: [subject line]
Subject Line 3: [subject line]

For each subject line, write 3 preview text options (40-80 characters each) that:
- Complete or extend the subject line (not just restate it)
- Add a secondary reason to open
- Create additional curiosity or urgency
- Feel like part of the same thought, not a separate sentence

Also: Which subject line + preview text combination do you recommend testing first, 
and why?
```

## Section 6: Building Your Email Swipe File and Prompt Library

### The Swipe File

A swipe file is a collection of email examples that inspire you — emails you received and loved, campaigns you admire, subject lines that made you open immediately. Professionals have maintained swipe files for decades; AI makes them more useful than ever.

**Building your swipe file:**
1. Create a dedicated folder in your email client for great emails you receive
2. When you see an email that makes you think "I wish I had written that," save it
3. When you complete a campaign that performs exceptionally, save those emails too
4. Review your swipe file quarterly and note why each example works

**Using your swipe file with AI:**

```prompt
Here is an email from my swipe file that I love:

[paste the email]

Tell me:
1. What psychological mechanisms make this email effective?
2. What structural patterns could I replicate?
3. What voice/tone techniques work especially well here?
4. Write a version of this for my brand and audience, keeping the structure 
   and psychology but completely changing the topic and content to [my topic]:

My topic: [describe]
My audience: [describe]
My brand voice: [describe]
```

### Your Email Prompt Library

Build a library of prompts for every email type you send regularly:

**Must-have prompt templates to build:**
- Welcome sequence emails (5-6 variations)
- Weekly newsletter structure
- Promotional launch emails (5-email sequence template)
- Re-engagement campaign (3-email sequence)
- Milestone emails (subscriber anniversary, purchase anniversary)
- Transactional email upgrades (shipping notifications, receipt follow-ups)
- Testimonial/case study request email
- Webinar invitation and reminder sequence

```prompt
I want to build a reusable prompt template for my [email type].

Here's an email of this type that I was happy with:
[paste your best example of this email type]

Convert this into a reusable prompt template with:
1. [PLACEHOLDER] variables clearly labeled (what changes each time)
2. The fixed structural and voice elements (what stays the same)
3. A brief header comment explaining when and how to use this template
4. Estimated time to customize (should be under 10 minutes per email)

Also: What information should I gather BEFORE running this prompt to 
get the best results?
```

> 🔍 **Case Study:** A B2B SaaS company's head of email marketing built a library of 22 prompt templates covering every email type they send. Before building the library, writing a new email campaign took 2-3 hours. After, it takes 25-40 minutes (brief + AI generation + voice editing). The 22 templates took approximately 12 hours to build. The first month after completing the library, they sent 31% more campaigns — without increasing team time. Open rates remained stable, and revenue from email increased by 28%.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Subject lines are the highest-leverage element** — a 5% improvement in open rate multiplies the value of every email you send; invest disproportionate attention here.

2. **Preview text is an overlooked conversion lever** — treat it as a second subject line, not an afterthought; 40-80 characters can meaningfully change open rates.

3. **The EMAIL framework structures conversion** — Engage, Message, Application, Invitation, Link gives every email a clear architecture that moves readers from reading to acting.

4. **Different email types serve different goals** — newsletters build relationship, nurture emails build consideration, launch sequences drive revenue; match the purpose to the content.

5. **AI drafts require voice editing** — the five common AI voice problems (over-formal openers, vague generalizations, predictable structure, missing personality, generic CTAs) are fixable with a deliberate editing pass.

6. **A swipe file is your most valuable creative resource** — collect the emails that make you think "I wish I wrote that" and use them to teach AI your aspirational standard.

7. **A prompt library is your productivity multiplier** — every great prompt saved is 2-3 hours you never have to spend again; build yours deliberately over time.
